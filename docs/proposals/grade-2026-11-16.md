# Arborous graded against its goals

Asked 2026-11-03, widened 2026-11-10, run 2026-11-16 and 17 from
`docs/prompts/grade-the-app.md`. Every grade and judgment here is the
grading session's own (the owner, 2026-11-16: "every judgment and grade is
yours"); agents only gathered. Nothing in the app was changed.

The lecture files graded are the owner's own, the three BIOL 365 lectures
they are studying for Midterm 2, with their slides (the owner, 2026-11-16:
"Grade the ones I actually made and am studying with"). Nothing from them is
quoted here: the repo is public. The details that name a card or a slide
are in a separate note handed to the owner, outside the repo.

## The short version

**What is strong.** The questions. On three real lectures they ask a
student to use what they learned, the wrong options are real mistakes, and
the feedback after an answer (each option with its reason, the
explanation, the passage) is the best thing in the app. The teacher's talk
reaches the file in words: the owner's handwriting is carried almost whole.
The book look holds on a phone and a desktop and feels made. The tone is
kind and never nags. The simulator habit has kept bad ideas out.

**The three gaps that matter most.**

1. **The time does not fit the material, and the app never says so.** One
   of these lectures is two to three hours of first pass. The three for
   Midterm 2 are about eight hours before a single review. A week of Today
   at the default 30 minutes reaches 7 of one lecture's 15 sections. No
   screen puts the daily time next to the exam, and the time there is goes
   down the slides in order, not to what matters most.
2. **Two fifths of Learn is reading a definition and typing its term**, and
   the definitions are written as riddles so that the typing is fair. The
   owner's rule of 2026-10-26 ("never read and then name straight away") is
   broken again in 11 steps of these three lectures.
3. **The figures are described and not shown.** Six slides in ten carry a
   figure. The files hold 58 notes on figures and 2 drawings. The Diagrams
   page of a real lecture is twelve phone screens of text about pictures
   that are not there.

Under all three is one cause, which is the main finding about how the app
is built: **the AI that makes a lecture decides things the file never
records, and the app guesses them back from the wording.** What each card
is about, how much the idea matters, which explanation a question rests on,
which slide it came from. Section 4 has the argument (gap 4) and section 5
the path.

**Round two** (section 10, 2026-11-17) leaves the prompt and the files
out and argues the seven most fundamental reworks of the app itself.

Also found, small and worth doing first: **a library with one lecture shows
no Today at all** (every new student's first days), and **one class was
split in two** on the owner's own library because two files spell its code
differently.

## 0. How this was decided

The owner's method: plan how to decide before deciding.

**The yardstick.** Three choices: grade each part against its own Goal line
in `docs/EXPECTATIONS.md`; grade everything against the intent in CLAUDE.md
alone; or both, part by part. Goal lines alone would pass a part that does
its small job while the app misses its purpose (a lecture check that runs
and is wrong). The intent alone gives no way to grade Backup. **Picked:
each part against its own goal, then the seven lenses of the brief where
they bear on it, and where the two disagree the intent wins and the report
says so.** The fourteen goals still marked "to confirm" are graded against
their drafts and marked *goal unconfirmed*.

**What counts as evidence**, strongest first: what a student meets on the
built app at phone size with real lectures loaded (walked, logged card by
card, screenshots); the app's own code run on the real files (its Learn
order, its lecture check, its card times); the simulator, in direction and
size only, under both simulated students; the code read by me; the history.
A grade that rests on reading alone, or on a made-up test lecture, says so.
The owner's own study log is not used (2026-11-03).

**The scale**, as the brief gives it: *Hits it · Mostly · Partly · Misses ·
Works against it*. A number was considered and rejected: it would look
more exact than the evidence is.

**The order.** By how often a student meets a part (the design audit's
rule): the daily path first (library, Today, a sitting, its end), then the
weekly (lecture page, Practice, the test, Progress), then the rare. The
lecture files came with the daily path, since every card is one.

**What was done.**

- The three lectures (794 items) and their 14 slide decks (313 slides) kept
  outside the repo. I read one deck page by page against its sections and a
  second from a written-out copy; the rest were written out as text by
  agents and checked by a rough word match, not slide by slide. The agents
  ran out of usage before finishing their tables, and the owner then asked
  for the rest of the app first (2026-11-17: comparing notes to files
  "takes a lot more usage"). So the slide-by-slide claim covers about 40
  slides, and the counts cover all 313.
- The built app driven at 390 by 844 with touch on: the three lectures
  uploaded through the app's own door, a first day of Today to its end, the
  library four hours later, the next day's sitting, then three days
  skipped; the first run, New lecture, Practice, a 20-question practice
  test to its results, Progress, Diagrams, Glossary, a class page, Account,
  Help; desktop at 1280 and 1920; load times with the processor slowed four
  times.
- `npm run sim` (42 scenarios, 3 seeds, level with the baseline), the
  real-lecture walk (`sim:lecture`), `npm run build`, about 1,100 tests and
  the linter, all passing.
- Read in full by me: the schema, the memory model, how a sitting is built
  (`buildSessionItems`), sync's merge, the generator prompt. Read in
  outline: the session store, Learn's steps, the practice test, the lecture
  check. Not read: the tree's drawing code, graded from the screen only.

**What was not done, so no grade leans on it.** No real round trip through
an AI chat (Add material, Add questions, Add explanations, Fix, Even out).
No real iPhone or iPad: everything is Chromium at phone size. Sync against
the server. Touch on the tree. Problems, studies and written answers barely
occur in these three lectures (no problems, six written cards), so those
kinds are graded by reading. The class is one biology class with annotated
slides: "built for any class" is argued from the code, not shown on a
second class.

## 2. The evolution: what the history shows

438 commits since 2026-08-31. 175 of them fell in two weeks of September
(the React rebuild, the book look, Learn, Today, the simulator, sync). Since
mid-October the work has been one ask at a time, each with its fix and its
rule.

**The pattern in the owner's corrections.** 23 commits cite the owner
correcting something. They fall into five kinds, and each says something
about how the app is checked:

1. **Built unasked, or the opposite of what was meant.** Written answers in
   every practice test; the test's marking changed on 10-21, four times on
   10-26 and again on 11-03. A session filled a gap in the ask with its own
   idea and shipped it.
2. **There, but a student could not find it or it did nothing.** The kind
   choice inside Practice "looked for and never found"; related terms that
   looked like buttons for weeks.
3. **Checked where it was easy, not where the student is.** The iPhone
   header, three attempts that passed in emulation; three asks that looked
   unfixed because the owner's phone ran an old copy.
4. **One class's needs became the app's defaults.** The studies treatment;
   wording that said slides.
5. **Lost between sessions.** Shuffled options, asked before records were
   kept; the "one agent at a time" rule nobody made.

What they have in common: each was fixed where it showed, as a rule in
Learn's ordering or a line in the prompt, and checked on a made-up test
lecture and a screenshot. None was checked by walking a real-sized lecture
as a student. So the same kind of miss comes back in a new place. It has:

- **"Never read and then name" is back.** Fixed 2026-10-26 by ordering each
  step. The fix has fallbacks for steps with no question of their own, and
  on real lectures those fallbacks fire 11 times. The tests that guard the
  rule run on the made-up test lectures, none of them shaped like these.
- **"Compare with" is the related-terms story again.** Repaired so a tap
  does something; on real lectures it appears under fewer than one term in
  ten, because nothing asks the AI for neighbours.
- **A design change that never shows.** The lighter reading card (design
  audit 3B, live 2026-11-06) folds the passage only on a definition with
  an example sentence. Not one of the 253 definitions in these lectures has
  one. The audit said to check it on a real lecture first; it was not.
- **The lecture check cries wolf** on good lectures, as the weak-points
  review called its warnings "wallpaper" in October. They were grouped and
  folded; they are still wrong.
- **The screenshot check cannot run in a new session.** The session hook
  installs one version of the browser driver and the machine carries
  another, so `scripts/screenshots.mjs` stops at once. The design audit
  found the script three weeks out of date; today it does not start.

**Decisions that could have gone another way.**

- **A definition is also a quiz (2026-09-25).** It made half of every file
  scoreable at no cost to the generator, a real gain then. It also tied the
  text a student learns from to the cue they are tested with, and every
  later rule about give-aways, spacing and "describe the other parts by
  their role" follows from that. This is gap 2.
- **Guess from the wording instead of asking the AI (2026-10-13).** When
  the first real study found repeats and give-aways, the fix was word
  overlap in the app (`contentChecks`), because lectures already existed
  and could not be asked. Since then Learn's steps, the Why under a
  question, the lecture check and "the same fact twice" all rest on shared
  words. The narration work of 2026-11-06 took the other road for the
  first time ("part of the map goes into the file") and it worked. This is
  gap 4.
- **The practice test as its own page (2026-10-15).** It began as
  multiple choice only, so a separate page was cheap. It then gained every
  kind of card, each written a second time, and was reshaped at least six times.
- **A memory model made here.** `lib/memory.ts` says of itself that its
  shape and constants are "modelling choices, not findings". The simulator's
  truth student is an open, fitted scheduler. The app's own model was
  simpler to start with and has waited two months for data to tune it.
- **Progress hangs on names the AI invents.** A card's score is kept under
  the id the generator gave it, so a better prompt cannot reach a studied
  lecture except through patch prompts. Hence the owner's "let me know when
  I'm good to regenerate".
- **Explain it in Help.** `HelpRoute` is the most changed file in the repo
  (84 commits). Much of the app's clarity was bought there instead of on
  the screen where the question arises.

**What the earlier reviews found, and what became of it.** Of about 60
findings in the five reviews and the design audit, nearly all are fixed,
checked here finding by finding against the code and the commits. Still
open:

- reviews late after a miss (learning audit, September): half built as the
  same-day catch-up, the rest waiting on a study log;
- the one-sitting-a-day student in exam week (fresh eyes, October);
- an exam whose ticked sections no longer exist counts as covering
  everything (code audit, low);
- generation cost (weak points 6): replies were made compact, and the
  prompt has grown from 42,000 to 62,000 characters since;
- the reading share on a first day (weak points 7), set aside on
  2026-10-15 because it rested on one study-heavy class. Three lectures of
  another class now show 41 to 46% reading. That is the new evidence, and
  gap 2 reopens it.

**What none of them looked at.** The daily time against the exam. The
figures. A library with one lecture. The whole first run with a real AI
chat, on a phone (not done here either). What a number means from one
screen to the next. Thirty lectures instead of three.

## 4. The top gaps: options, argued

### Gap 1. The time and the material

What it is for: "a student who just presses Today each day, for the time
they have, ends up as ready as that time allows". Who meets it: everyone,
daily. Evidence: the card times the app itself uses put a first pass of
these lectures at 124, 173 and 163 minutes; `sim:lecture` on one of them at
30 minutes a day reached 7 of 15 sections and 85 of 249 cards in a week;
across the simulator's suite the daily time moves the exam score more than
any feature ever tested (47, 66, 82% at 30, 45, 60 minutes). The default is
30 minutes a lecture, so three lectures are offered as 90. And the owner,
studying these lectures on 2026-11-17, found the same from the other side:
Learn beside the notes "took far longer" than any plan had assumed. The
app's card times are for answering a card, not for looking something up in
the notes on the way, so its 30 minutes run long as well as short of the
material.

- **A. Leave it.** The student sets the minutes. But they cannot know that
  30 is far too few, and the app's whole promise is that they need not know.
- **B. A pace line.** Where an exam is set: "At 30 minutes a day you reach
  section 9 of 15 by Midterm 2. About 50 reaches all of it", with the
  minutes one tap away. With no exam: how many days the lecture takes at
  this pace. Arithmetic on cards left and measured seconds per card, which
  the app already has. This is not the readiness forecast that failed in
  September: that predicted memory; this counts cards. Against: it can
  read as pressure. It should state a fact and offer a choice, in the
  app's voice, and never count days missed.
- **C. Smaller lectures.** Tell the prompt to write fewer cards. Against:
  it gives up the file's own bar (nothing on the exam unpractised), and the
  cards are good. The fault is not that they exist.
- **D. What matters most first, when the time will not reach.** The
  generator already sorts every idea into key, supporting, background and
  illustration detail, then discards it. Written into the file, Today could
  teach each section's key ideas first when the pace line says not all of
  it fits, still in the slides' order, leaving background terms for last.
  This is the owner's "efficient on high-yield content without losing the
  nuance". Against: it bends "in the slides' order" (it keeps the order of
  sections, not of every card), it needs the file to carry weight (gap 4),
  and lectures already made need it added.

**Pick: B now, D after the simulator.** B is small, honest, and changes no
schedule. D is the real answer to "as ready as that time allows", but it
changes what Today teaches and must be modelled first.

Cost: B, a day or two (one line on the library, the lecture page and the
exam dialog, from numbers the app already computes). D, a week: a field in
the file and the prompt, a rule in Today, an "add weights" prompt for
lectures already made.

Before building D, the simulator must show, under both students: with the
exam scored by weight, a 30-minute student scores higher with key ideas
first, and a 60-minute student, who reaches everything anyway, scores no
lower. And a real sitting read start to finish must still follow the
lecture.

### Gap 2. Terms, and the definition that does two jobs

What it is for: Learn teaches "the way the teacher did"; "understand, not
memorise". Evidence: in a first pass 18 to 21% of the time is reading
definitions and 17 to 23% typing their terms; the longest run of typed
terms in a row is 7; 11 steps read and then only name. And the wording:
the prompt tells the AI that a definition must not contain its term or a
word of its family, and must describe other terms "by their role" so no
card answers another. The result, on every lecture, is a definition
written around its subject. A made-up case of the pattern: instead of "the
valve between the left atrium and the left ventricle", the card says "the
flap between the upper and lower chambers on the side that feeds the
body". That sentence is the student's first meeting with the idea, and it
is also what the Glossary shows.

- **A. Patch the order again.** Join a step that only reads and names to
  the next one. Small. But it is the fix of 2026-10-26 a second time, and
  it leaves the riddles and the hours.
- **B. Give the two jobs to two things.** The definition is written plainly,
  to teach, and may name its neighbours. The quiz cue is made by the app:
  the definition with the term and its family blanked, or the AI's own
  short clue in a second field. And typing a term moves out of the first
  meeting: in Learn a term is read, then used in the questions that follow,
  and is first typed in the next day's review, where it is recall and not
  an echo. Against: the simulator scored multiple choice before typing
  lower on 2026-10-26 (kept as the owner's order), so moving it further may
  score lower still; and a blanked definition can be clumsy.
- **C. Terms off by default.** The student can already untick "Terms to
  name". Against: producing a name is real practice (docs/evidence.md), and
  some exams ask for it.
- **D. Type only the terms that matter.** Key and supporting terms are
  typed, background ones read. Needs weight in the file.

**Pick: B, with D when weight exists.** It answers the owner's rule at its
root, gives back the plain sentence, and takes nothing away for good: every
term is still typed, a day later. The evidence file already has "the first
test should wait a little", which is the case for it.

Cost: about a week. The prompt (a plain definition, an optional clue); the
app's cue; Learn's step without the typing; Review asking a term first.
Lectures already made keep working: their definitions simply stay as they
are until Fix or a regeneration.

Before building, the simulator must show both students no lower on exam
morning and more sections reached per week (the time freed should buy
coverage); and a real Learn sitting must be read to check that no question
names a term the student has not met.

### Gap 3. The figures

What it is for: "a diagram the slide's text talks about is the point, to be
known"; "even if they never … opened the slides". Evidence: 189 of 313
slides carry a figure; 58 figure notes, 2 drawings; a note may say to
follow a coloured line that the student cannot see.

`docs/proposals/narration.md` decided on 2026-11-06 that a note and a
pointer beat a crude copy. I do not overturn that. The new evidence is how
much of a real lecture is figure, and there is a third way that was not on
the table then.

- **A. Leave it.** The note in words, and "in your own notes: page 4".
  Fine at a desk with the slides open. On a bus it is a caption without
  its picture.
- **B. Have the AI draw more.** Already judged: a redrawn anatomical plate
  is a crude copy, and wrong ones would be scored against.
- **C. Bring the slides into the app.** The student adds the lecture's
  PDF once. It stays on the device (never in a share link, never in the
  public repo's world). A figure's card and plate show the real page. The
  card that opens a section, which now says "read this part of your own
  material", opens those pages. Later, any card can show its slide.
  Against: a new store (the files are 1 to 12 MB, too big for where
  lectures live now), a PDF reader to load (loaded only when used), pages
  that hold three slides each in a OneNote export, and it does nothing for
  a student who made the lecture from pasted text.

**Pick: C, in three steps that each ship.** (1) The file names the page of
each figure as a number, not only in words, and the app shows that page on
the figure's card and plate when the PDF is there. (2) Learn's opening card
opens the section's pages. (3) A slide per card.

Cost: step 1 is about a week and is where the risk is (storage and the
reader); steps 2 and 3 are days each. What a real sitting must show first:
on a phone, the page readable at the size it gets, and the app no slower
to open with three PDFs stored.

### Gap 4. What the file knows, and what the app guesses

This is the systems finding, and it explains several others.

In making a lecture the AI decides, for every card: which idea it is about;
how much that idea matters; which side of the idea it asks (name it, say
it, tell it apart, use it, predict, explain why: the prompt's own list);
which explanation it rests on; which slide it came from. None of this is
written down. The app then rebuilds it from shared words:

- **Which explanation is a question's Why**: "the explanation most of its
  words are in". On the real lectures 46 of 89, 74 of 123 and 31 of 118
  questions get one.
- **Whether a question asks for more than recall**: a list of trigger words
  ("if", "blocked", "versus"). It counts 15 of 90, 24 of 115 and 22 of 106
  on lectures where most questions clearly do. `narration.md` flagged this
  measure for this session: it is not fit to raise a warning on.
- **Which step a card belongs to, and what gives what away**: term names
  and word overlap, about 1,500 lines across `learnSteps`,
  `contentChecks` and `questionQuality`. Learn's steps and the builder of
  a sitting have been changed 24 and 44 times.
- **How much an idea matters**: not available at all. "Weighting by
  importance" sits on the back burner waiting for the owner's study log,
  while the generator has the answer in hand.

- **A. Keep guessing, tune the guesses.** No change to files. But every
  tuning so far has been bought with an owner correction, and wording
  differs by class: a maths or a code lecture shares few words between a
  question and its idea.
- **B. Part of the plan goes into the file**, as the narration did. Each
  card carries, in a few characters: the idea it is about, the side it
  asks, the page it came from. Each idea carries its weight. An
  explanation carries the ideas it explains. Against: a longer reply (a
  few per cent), labels the AI can get wrong, and two paths in the app
  while old lectures remain.
- **C. A new file format.** Ideas at the top, cards under them. Cleaner,
  and it would strand every lecture already made.

**Pick: B.** Optional fields, so every existing lecture still loads and
falls back to the guesses. Then replace one guess at a time with the label
where a lecture has it: first the Why, then the lecture check's "recall
only", then weight for gap 1, then the page for gap 3. Each step ships
alone and is checked on a real lecture.

Cost: the fields and the prompt, two or three days; each replacement, a day
or two. What must be shown first: one real lecture made with the new
prompt on Opus 5.5 at high effort, read card by card for whether the
labels are right, before anything in the app trusts them.

### Gap 5. The first days

- **The library with one lecture has no Today.** One line of code decides
  it. A new student opens the app and sees Upload, New lecture, a search
  box and a row. Show the lecture's Today on the library whenever there is
  one. Small, and first.
- **The class.** Match class codes ignoring spaces and letter case, and let
  New lecture offer the classes the student already has, as the owner
  suggested on 2026-11-11. It then stops depending on the AI's spelling.
- **The trip to the AI.** The owner's ask of 2026-11-16, a file back and
  never pasted text, removes the worst half of it on a phone. Beyond that,
  one idea to weigh and not to build yet: the prompt as a file the student
  attaches beside their slides ("attach these two things"), so nothing
  62,000 characters long is ever copied.

### Gap 7. The first look after a miss

The simulator has said since September that the review after a miss comes
too late for both simulated students, that no change to the thresholds
fixes it under both, and that the same-day catch-up helps. The catch-up
only happens if the student comes back, and the app has no way to ask them
to. Two honest options: a reminder at the time the app already names on
the end screen ("worth one more look after 10:31 PM"), which is the first
thing the app would ever send and so is the owner's to allow; or take the
after-miss behaviour of the open, fitted scheduler the simulator already
uses as its student, with a different truth student to test it against. I
would not act on the second without the first being decided. It is a
question at the end.

## 5. The systems

For each: its shape now, the shape it should have, and whether to go there.

**The lecture file (`src/schema`).** *Mostly.* Loose, forgiving, optional
fields with careful fallbacks; a broken card never rejects a lecture. Two
things are encoded by convention where they should be stated: a written
answer is a flashcard whose back happens to be numbered or whose tags say
so, and a long answer can be an example whose title matches a pattern. And
it carries none of the links of gap 4. *Go there*: the optional fields of
gap 4, and a stated kind for written answers when the file next changes.

**What is stored (`src/store`).** *Mostly.* Lectures compressed, a key
each; the three real lectures took 82,000 of about 5.2 million characters,
so there is room for a hundred. Scores are kept by the AI's
card ids. *Go there, one thing*: when a lecture is regenerated, carry the
scores across by matching cards (a term by its name, a question by its
wording), so a better prompt can reach a lecture already studied. Gap 3
needs a second store for files; build it there, not before.

**The memory model (`lib/memory`).** *Partly.* Small and readable, exam
aware, fitted to the student's pace. Its constants are untested guesses by
its own account, the docs say a miss "resets to the strength of first
learning" where the code halves it, and it reads a hidden global for the
student's forgetting rate. *Do not rewrite now*: it waits on gap 7's
question and on data.

**Building a sitting (`lib/buildSessionItems`, `lib/learnSteps`,
`store/session`).** *Partly.* One 370-line function builds twelve modes,
several of which now overlap with what Practice's ticks can ask for;
Learn's order is a pipeline of repairs (`apart`, `withQuestions`,
`reordered`, `joinSmall`). It works, and every owner correction to Learn
landed here as another repair. *Go there*: after gap 4, a card's place can
come from its idea and its side, and most of the repairs retire. Until then
add the one check that would have caught section 2's misses: the walk of a
real-sized made-up lecture, with "no step only reads and names" as a test.

**The kinds of card (`components/items`) and the practice test
(`routes/TestRoute`).** *Partly.* Each kind is named in 23 to 30 files and
53 to 70 places; adding select all touched about 30. The practice test asks
and marks every kind in its own copy (1,589 lines), sharing the small
pieces. *Go there, gradually*: one description per kind (how it is asked,
how it is marked, how it reads once marked, how it is edited, what it
counts as) that the sitting, the test and Browse all use. Not a rewrite:
move one kind at a time, starting with the next kind anyone adds, and stop
if it does not get easier.

**The lecture check (`lib/courseHealth`).** *Partly.* See the scorecard.
*Go there*: after gap 4 the checks that guess become checks that read a
label. Before then, take the two findings shown wrong here out of what a
student sees.

**The prompts.** *Mostly.* One spec, cut per use, a good idea. It has
drifted from the app: it still tells the AI the old order in a step (type
each term straight after reading), says only multiple choice and flashcards
are graded, and calls the app a flashcard app. The AI checks its own draft
against an order the app no longer uses. The owner's ask of 2026-11-17 (the
prompt should tell the AI how the app uses each kind) is half met already:
the section exists, and it is out of date. *Fix it with that ask.*

**Sync and backup.** *Mostly.* A pure, tested merge; whole state in one
row per student, read, merged and written whole. Fine at this size. At
thirty lectures that is megabytes on every open; split it per lecture when
it is felt, not before.

**The tree.** *Mostly, from the screen.* About 2,000 lines I did not read.
It draws well and tells the truth.

**The offline app and updates.** *Mostly, by reading EXPECTATIONS.* The
old-copy problem that hid three fixes from the owner is handled; still
waiting on the owner's own check.

**The simulator, the tests, the checks.** *Mostly.* About 1,100 tests and a
simulator with rules worth keeping. What they miss is named in section 2:
nothing walks a real-sized lecture, nothing opens the built app in a
browser before it ships, and the screenshot script does not run in a new
session. *Go there*: pin the browser driver to the one the machine has;
add a short browser run to the deploy (open each page, finish one sitting,
take one test); add the long made-up lecture.

## 7. What a person notices

## 10. Round two: the reworks of the app itself

Asked 2026-11-17: only the app, its major systems, and whether each rests
on the right idea for what the app is for. The prompt, the lecture file's
structure and the slides are left out, and each lecture file is taken as
given. Small fixes are skipped. Every judgment is mine; nothing was built.

**How this round was decided.** The yardstick is CLAUDE.md's first
paragraph: a lesson plan in which each thing builds on the last, as a tutor
would teach it, in the slides' order, adapting to gaps, efficient on what
matters, testing understanding, and getting the student there even if they
never went. A system is a rework here only if its *idea* misses that, not
its details. Evidence: the code read by me (how Today splits a day, the
state of a sitting, the study log, how Learn cuts its steps, the routes),
the round-one walk on a phone, and three new simulator runs on one of the
owner's lectures with an exam ten days off. Costs are for one session
working with the simulator and the checks, and are rough.

**The seven, in order of what they change for a student.**

| # | Rework | The idea it replaces |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The plan runs to the exam | Today plans one day of one lecture |
| 2 | Learn teaches ideas through questions | Learn is the term list, four at a time |
| 3 | Home is the student's term | The screens follow the file |
| 4 | One engine for every sitting, with a clock | A sitting is a list built once; the test is a second engine |
| 5 | Keep what happened, not only what was concluded | A card's memory is a stored summary |
| 6 | Storage and sync sized for a whole degree | Everything in one small store, synced whole |
| 7 | Code sorted by what the app does | Code sorted by technical layer |

**What rests on the right idea and should not be reworked:** Review asking
what is due, most urgent first, with the exam steering it; Practice and the
practice test as two jobs; a miss coming back inside its step and again a
few hours on; local first, with sync optional; the simulator deciding
before a screen is built.

### Rework 1. The plan runs to the exam, not to the end of the day

**The root.** Today plans one day, for one lecture: everything due, then new
material, inside the minutes set (`lib/today` splitSitting). The exam
enters only in its last four days, as a cap on review. The app never works
out what the whole run to an exam costs. So it cannot tell a student their
minutes are short while there is still time to change them, and three
lectures on one exam are three separate plans, started one after another.

The numbers, on one of the owner's lectures with the exam ten days away
(`sim:lecture`, a week of Today): at 30 minutes a day, 7 of 15 sections
reached; at 60 minutes, 12 of 15. From the third day about two thirds of
every sitting is review, because each new card is due again the next day
and three more times in the nine days after. That schedule is right for
holding what was learned; the simulator found on 2026-09-26 that cutting
review all month is much worse. So no scheduling trick finds the time. The
time has to be asked for, or the scope chosen, and today the app does
neither. That exam covers three such lectures.

**The options.**

- **A. A pace line** (round one's pick): say what the daily time reaches.
  Small and honest, but it reads the plan out; it does not make one.
- **B. Budget to the exam.** For everything an exam covers, across its
  lectures, the app estimates the whole cost: the first meetings, plus the
  reviews its own memory model will ask for before the day. Divided by the
  days left, that is the daily time that reaches it. Today becomes one day
  of that plan, and a missed day quietly re-divides what is left. Against:
  the estimate rests on a memory model that is not calibrated, so it must
  be shown as "about", and checked against the simulator.
- **C. The student chooses the trade when the time is short.** More
  minutes, or which sections first. The simulator's result gives the rule
  for the second: a part learned and reviewed beats the whole seen once.
  The exam dialog already has the ticks for what an exam covers; this adds
  an order.
- **D. The full rework: one Today for an exam, across its lectures.**
  Review mixed from all of them, new material from the lecture whose turn
  it is, one sitting. Against: Learn's steps and Today's plan are built per
  lecture (`init` takes one lecture; only Practice can mix), so this is
  the largest piece.

**Pick: B and C, then D.** The exam becomes the thing that is planned.
A alone leaves the student to do the arithmetic every day.

**What it unlocks.** A student sets the exam once and sees, on the first
day, "about 55 minutes a day reaches all of it; at your 30, this much".
They choose. Nobody finds out on the last night.

**Cost.** B four or five days, C two or three, D one to two weeks.

**Path.** (1) The estimate as a pure function, with a simulator check that
it lands within a fifth of what the simulated student needed, under both
students. Shown nowhere yet. (2) Shown on the class page beside each exam.
(3) On the library and the lecture page. (4) The order of sections. (5)
The cross-lecture Today behind the "All 3 lectures" Start that exists,
which today chains them.

### Rework 2. Learn teaches ideas through questions

**The root.** A section's steps are its definitions, in file order, cut
four at a time. Every question, explanation and figure is then hung on the
step of a term it names (`lib/learnSteps` stepSection). So the skeleton of
Learn is a vocabulary list. Half of its cards are a definition read or
typed. A section opens with up to six cards of reading before the first
question. And "read, then name" returns wherever a step has no question
of its own.

What the app is for is the opposite order of importance: the connections
first, the names along the way. The files already carry the connections:
each section says what it builds to, and has explanations and questions
graded easy to hard.

Two runs to size it. With the terms taken out of the first pass entirely,
the same week at 30 minutes reached 9 sections against 7: an upper bound on
what moving them buys in reach. "Skip the reading", which exists today,
reached 8, with 20 to 29 misses a sitting against 5 to 25: asking cold
costs most of what it saves.

**The options.**

- **A. Keep the term skeleton and patch it.** That is what every Learn
  correction since October has done.
- **B. A step is an idea.** It opens with the explanation or the figure,
  asks a question that needs it, then the others from easy to hard. The
  terms it uses are read in passing, each one tap from its meaning, as the
  glossary beside a sitting now allows on a computer. Typing a term moves
  to the next day's review, where it is recall. Sections that have no
  explanation keep today's shape. Against: with the file as given, which
  questions belong to which explanation is still the app's guess from
  shared words, and it finds one for between a quarter and three fifths of
  questions on the real lectures; the rest fall back.
- **C. Questions first by default.** Try, then be told. The evidence
  supports trying first, and it suits a student who has read the slides.
  It is wrong as the default for the student the owner names, the one who
  never went: the run above shows how many more misses it means.
- **D. The full rework: Learn adapts inside the sitting.** Right first
  time on an idea's harder questions, and its easy cards go straight to
  review. Missed, and the explanation reopens and a second question on the
  same idea comes from another side, now, not next sitting. This is the
  "coming back to what they struggled with" of the owner's first prompt,
  and it is not possible while a sitting is a fixed list (rework 4).

**Pick: B, then D.** B is the change of idea; D is what makes it a tutor.

This touches two of the owner's own lines: "no drastic changes to how it
teaches" (2026-10-21) and the order Read, Recall, Apply (2026-10-26). B
keeps that order inside each idea and changes what is read first and when
a term is typed. It needs the owner's yes before it is built.

**What it unlocks.** A first question within a card or two. A sitting that
feels like being taught one thing after another, not like a word list. More
of the lecture met in the same minutes.

**Cost.** B a week to ten days; D another week, after rework 4.

**Path.** A second step builder beside the first, chosen by a switch on the
device like "Skip the reading". The owner studies one lecture with it. Then
it becomes the default and the old builder stays for sections without
explanations. Before that: both simulated students no lower on exam
morning, and one real sitting read card by card.

### Rework 3. Home is the student's term, not a shelf of files

**The root.** The screens follow the file. The home page is "My lectures".
A class is a heading over files. An exam is a dialog. Progress belongs to
one lecture. A student thinks the other way round: Midterm 2 is on
Thursday, it covers three lectures, what do I do today, am I ready.

What follows from the file-first shape:

- The same three ways to study exist at four places (the library, a
  class, a lecture, a section), about a dozen doors.
- "Am I ready for the exam" is answered on no screen. Progress is per
  lecture, and the account page shows habits.
- The top of the page opened every day is Upload and New lecture.
- A library with one lecture has no Today. One class became two.

**The options.**

- **A. Leave the shape, fix the faults.**
- **B. Re-rank the home page.** Today first, always. Then the exams
  coming, each with its days left and its pace. Then the classes, folded,
  their lectures inside (the owner's ask of 2026-11-16). Adding a lecture
  moves into its class and to the foot.
- **C. An exam's own page.** What it covers, the plan (rework 1), where the
  student stands by lecture and section, its practice test, and Today.
  Progress becomes "where I stand for this exam", not one lecture's
  figures.
- **D. Navigation that stays.** Three places one tap away on every screen
  (Today, the library, the student's own page), set as a book's running
  foot. Against: a tab bar is the web furniture the design language
  avoids; this one is taste, and should be seen as a drawing first.
- **E. The full rework: scope is a choice, not a place.** One Practice
  page and one practice test, each with "From: this section, this
  lecture, this class, this exam", in place of a copy at each level.

**Pick: B and C, then E; D only if the owner likes it drawn.**

A note on the practice test, since it belongs on the exam's page. It
rehearses well. As a measure it has one flaw by design: a new test asks
first what was last missed (the owner, 2026-10-26: "ADAPTIVE"), so its
score runs low on purpose. Keep that. On the exam's page, say where the
student stands from the questions that were new to a test, not from the
whole score.

**What it unlocks.** Open the app: the exam, today's sitting, whether the
pace holds. One tap. Everything else is under its class.

**Cost.** B three or four days, C about a week, E about a week.

**Path.** B ships alone and changes no route. C is a new page that the
class page links to, with the old exam dialog kept for editing. E folds
the lecture and class copies of Practice and the test into one each, old
links still opening the right scope.

### Rework 4. One engine for every sitting, and it knows the clock

**The root.** A sitting is a list built once and a position in it
(`store/session`). Answers are kept by position. A miss comes back as a
copy spliced into the list, with every later position shifted. Time is not
part of it at all. The practice test is a second engine, with its own
state and its own copy of every kind of card.

What a student meets because of it: a sitting planned at 46 cards that
runs to 74; a count that changes under them; no way to say "I have ten
minutes"; and the test and a sitting drifting apart, so that shuffling,
"I don't know" and marking were each fixed twice, once per engine.

**The options.**

- **A. Leave it.**
- **B. A time box on the list.** Stop offering new steps when the minutes
  are spent. Cheap, and it would fix the overrun. It leaves two engines
  and a list that cannot adapt.
- **C. One engine.** A sitting is what has been asked, what came of it and
  the time used, plus a rule that picks the next card. Each way to study
  is one such rule: Learn's steps, Review's due order, Practice's ticks,
  the test's draw with the marking held back. Each kind of card is
  described once (how it is asked, marked, shown once marked, edited) and
  used by all of them. The simulator's policies already have this shape,
  so it could drive the same rules.
- **D. Rewrite the session and the test from nothing.** Rejected: about
  1,100 tests of behaviour would have to be earned again.

**Pick: C, one mode at a time.**

**What it unlocks.** Sittings that end on time at a sensible place. "I have
ten minutes." The adaptive Learn of rework 2. A test and a sitting that
always behave alike. Leaving and coming back that works the same
everywhere. A new kind of card built once.

**Cost.** Three to four weeks in all, the riskiest for regressions in this
list after rework 6.

**Path.** The engine first under Practice, the simplest rule. Then Review,
Today, Learn, and the practice test last. At each step the old code for the
modes not yet moved stays as it is, and the walk of a whole sitting plus a
run in a real browser must pass before the next.

### Rework 5. Keep what happened, not only what was concluded

**The root.** For each card the app stores a summary: right, wrong, last
seen, and a strength worked out by today's formula (`store/progress`). The
log of answers has no card in it, holds 800 answers a lecture, and is
trimmed when storage fills (`store/studyLog`). So:

- The memory model can never be refitted or replaced without leaving every
  studied card on the old numbers. Its constants are guesses by its own
  account, and several open items wait on calibrating them.
- That calibration has one student's thin log to use, which the owner
  rightly distrusts.
- Sync can only keep the later of two summaries.
- The app cannot use a card's history: missed three times the same way,
  sure and wrong, slow and right.

On whether the model itself rests on the right idea: its curve and its
exam steering do. What it lacks is any way to be checked, and it treats a
right answer picked from four options the same as one typed from nothing.

**The options.**

- **A. Leave it and wait for the owner's log.**
- **B. Add the card to the log and lift the cap.** Needs room (rework 6).
- **C. The answers are the truth.** Each answer is kept: the card, when,
  right or wrong, how it was given (picked, typed, flipped), the grade,
  sure or not, the seconds. A card's strength is worked out from its
  answers by the current model and kept only as a cache. A better model
  replays them.
- **D. Swap in the open, fitted scheduler now.** Against: it is the
  simulator's own student, so the simulator could no longer judge it; the
  September tests flipped direction by student; and without C it is one
  more set of frozen numbers.

**Pick: C.** Then two uses of it. First, let the model weigh how an answer
was given, tested in the simulator. Second, the owner's decision because it
reaches outside the app: let students who agree share their answer logs
without names, so the model is fitted on many students and not one.

**What it unlocks.** The schedule can improve for lectures already studied.
The items on the back burner come off it. A card can show its own history.
Two devices merge by adding answers together, losing none.

**Cost.** About a week for the log and the replay; days for each use.

**Path.** Start writing full answers now, beside the summaries: no change a
student sees. Prove in tests that replaying them gives the stored
summaries. Then the summaries become the cache. Only then try a different
model, in the simulator and on the replayed logs.

### Rework 6. Storage and sync sized for a whole degree

**The root.** Everything lives in the browser's small text store, about
five million characters. Because of that the lectures are compressed, the
log is capped and trimmed, and nothing large can be kept. Sync reads the
student's whole library as one record, merges it and writes it whole, each
time the app opens and closes (`lib/cloud` syncNow).

Three lectures fit with room for a hundred. What does not fit is the next
idea: a full answer history for a year of classes is more than the store
holds. And sixty lectures is megabytes up and down on every open, on a
phone's data.

**The options.**

- **A. Leave it until it is felt.**
- **B. The browser's database behind the same store, and sync by record.**
  A row per lecture with when it was last edited; answers added in
  batches, merged by union. Backups stay one plain file.
- **C. A ready-made sync service.** A dependency and a bill for a problem
  this size does not need.

**Pick: B, when rework 5 needs the room, not before.**

**What it unlocks.** Nothing to see on the first day. After it: no "storage
full" trimming, history kept for years, sync that sends only what changed,
room for whatever a later feature needs to keep.

**Cost.** One and a half to two weeks. It moves every student's data, which
makes it the riskiest change here.

**Path.** Read the old store and write both for a release. Check they
agree on open. Switch reads. Keep the old copy for a month, and a backup
prompt before the switch. A security review of the new tables.

### Rework 7. Code sorted by what the app does

**The root.** The code is sorted by technical layer: 85 files side by side
in `lib/`, pages of 500 to 1,600 lines that own behaviour, "which mode is
this" asked in 50 places, each kind of card named in 23 to 30 files. To
change how one way of studying works, a session must know to touch the
builder, the store, the component and two or three pages. Round one's
history section shows what that costs: fixes that landed in one place and
not its twin.

**The options.**

- **A. Leave it.**
- **B. Folders by what the app does:** the lecture, memory, the plan, the
  sitting, each kind of card, the practice test, the library, sync. Each
  with a small front door, and a lint rule against reaching past it.
- **C. One big move.** Rejected: weeks of churn with nothing for a
  student, and every open branch broken.

**Pick: B, done as each rework above touches an area, never on its own.**
With two guards added at once, since they are what the history says was
missing: a run of the built app in a real browser before each deploy (open
every page, finish a sitting, take a test), and the walk of a real-sized
made-up lecture.

**What it unlocks.** For a student, indirectly: fewer things fixed in one
place and not the other, and asks that land faster.

**Cost.** Two or three days for the guards. The moving costs little when it
rides on the other reworks.

### The order I would build them in

1. Rework 3's home page (B) and rework 1's estimate: both visible, both
   cheap, and they fix what a student meets first.
2. Rework 5's first step, writing full answers: invisible and cheap, and
   every day it waits is history not kept.
3. Rework 2's new Learn (B), on a switch.
4. Rework 4, mode by mode, with rework 7's guards from its first step.
5. Rework 6, then the rest of 5.
6. Rework 2's adaptive Learn (D) and rework 1's cross-lecture Today (D),
   which both need 4.
